Economic Contexts

Understanding how economic conditions, employment patterns, and financial pressures shape the environments in which children grow, learn, and thrive.

Economic contexts encompass the broad financial conditions that influence family life, including household income, employment stability, cost of living, and access to economic resources. In Australia, rising housing costs, wage stagnation, and the increasing prevalence of gig economy work have significantly altered the economic landscape for families with children.

Child poverty remains a pressing concern, with approximately 1 in 6 Australian children living below the poverty line. Economic disadvantage is not uniform — it intersects with geography, family structure, and cultural background to create complex patterns of vulnerability.

Household Income Employment Stability Cost of Living Gig Economy

Economic hardship has profound and well-documented effects on children's development. Research consistently shows that children growing up in low-income households are more likely to experience developmental delays, lower educational attainment, and poorer health outcomes. The stress of financial insecurity affects parental mental health, which in turn impacts parenting capacity and the quality of the home environment.

Material deprivation — the inability to access basic necessities such as nutritious food, adequate housing, and educational resources — directly limits children's opportunities. Social exclusion often accompanies economic disadvantage, creating barriers to participation in community activities, peer relationships, and cultural experiences that support healthy development.

Key Statistics

  • •Children in lowest income quintile are 2-3 times more likely to experience developmental vulnerability
  • •Financial stress correlates with higher rates of family conflict and relationship breakdown
  • •Housing instability disrupts school attendance and social connections

Australia's social policy framework includes a range of measures designed to support families experiencing economic disadvantage. The Family Tax Benefit system, including FTB-A and FTB-B, provides income-tested payments to assist with the costs of raising children. The Child Care Subsidy aims to improve workforce participation by reducing out-of-pocket child care costs.

Despite these interventions, gaps remain. The rate of JobSeeker and related payments has been widely criticised as inadequate, particularly for single parents. The National Affordable Housing Agreement has not met its targets, and waiting lists for public housing continue to grow. Recent inquiries, including the Senate committee on poverty, have called for a comprehensive redesign of the income support system.

Family Tax Benefit Child Care Subsidy JobSeeker Payment National Housing Agreement

Educators and practitioners working with economically disadvantaged families should adopt a strengths-based approach that recognises the resourcefulness and resilience many families demonstrate. Practical strategies include ensuring that early learning environments are genuinely inclusive and free from stigmatisation, providing fee relief information discreetly, and connecting families with financial counselling services.

Advocacy is a critical component of practice. This may involve supporting individual families to navigate complex welfare systems, or engaging in collective advocacy for policy change. Understanding the structural drivers of disadvantage helps practitioners avoid deficit-based thinking and instead focus on systemic barriers.

Effective responses to economic disadvantage require collaborative partnerships across sectors. Early childhood services, schools, community health centres, financial counselling services, and housing agencies all play important roles. Communities of Practice that bring together diverse professionals can enhance coordinated support for families.

Place-based initiatives — such as the Australian Government's Communities for Children program — demonstrate the value of locally-driven partnerships that respond to specific community needs. These models emphasise community voice and build on existing social capital rather than imposing external solutions.

Key resources include the AEDC (Australian Early Development Census) data, which provides community-level snapshots of children's development, the COAG Reform Council reports on productivity and social inclusion, and the Smith Family's research on disadvantage and education. For children, picture books and resources that normalise diverse family circumstances without pathologising economic difference are valuable additions to early learning environments.

Section 02

Social Contexts

Examining the social structures, relationships, and community dynamics that influence children's development and family functioning in contemporary Australia.

Social contexts refer to the web of relationships, institutions, and cultural norms that shape daily life. For children and families, this includes family structures, peer networks, community connections, digital environments, and broader social trends. Australian society has undergone significant social change — family structures are increasingly diverse, digital technology has transformed social interaction, and community ties have evolved in complex ways.

The concept of social capital is central to understanding social contexts. Bonding social capital (strong ties within groups) and bridging social capital (connections across groups) both contribute to family wellbeing. Communities with high social capital tend to have better outcomes for children, including improved safety, stronger educational engagement, and greater resilience during difficult times.

Social isolation and loneliness affect both children and adults, with significant consequences for mental health and development. For children, limited social networks can reduce opportunities for play-based learning, language development, and the formation of secure attachments beyond the family. For parents, social isolation is a known risk factor for postnatal depression, parenting stress, and child maltreatment.

Changing family structures — including the growth of single-parent families, blended families, and same-sex parented families — present both opportunities and challenges. While diversity in family form is increasingly accepted, families that deviate from the traditional model may still encounter social stigma, institutional barriers, and additional complexity in navigating support systems.

Australian social policy has increasingly recognised the importance of social connections. The National Framework for Protecting Australia's Children emphasises the role of community in child safety and wellbeing. The Child Care Package includes measures to support social inclusion, and the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) has transformed support for children with disability and their families.

Programs such as Parents as Teachers, Home Interaction Program for Parents and Youngsters (HIPPY), and Supported Playgroups demonstrate government investment in strengthening parent-child relationships and building social networks. The recognition of same-sex marriage in 2017 removed a significant legal barrier for many families, though challenges around assisted reproduction and parentage rights persist in some jurisdictions.

Practitioners should actively facilitate social connection rather than assuming it will occur naturally. This includes creating welcoming, inclusive environments that value diverse family structures, designing programs that bring families together in non-stigmatising ways, and supporting children's social skill development through guided play and peer interaction.

Digital literacy has become a critical practice area. Helping families navigate online environments safely, understanding the impact of screen time on development, and leveraging digital tools to maintain connections (particularly for geographically isolated families) are all essential competencies for contemporary practice.

Building genuine community partnerships requires moving beyond transactional relationships. Effective partnerships are characterised by shared goals, mutual respect, transparent communication, and distributed power. Early learning centres that function as community hubs — offering parent groups, health visiting services, and community events — can play a pivotal role in strengthening social fabric.

Interprofessional collaboration is equally important. Social workers, educators, health professionals, and community development workers each bring valuable perspectives. Models such as the Tasmanian Child and Family Centres demonstrate how co-located, integrated services can improve outcomes for families experiencing social isolation or complexity.

Recommended resources include the Australian Institute of Family Studies (AIFS) publications on family diversity, the Raising Children Network's evidence-based parenting resources, Early Childhood Australia's position statements on inclusion, and the eSafety Commissioner's resources for digital safety. For children, books that represent diverse family structures — such as those shortlisted for the Australian Book Industry Awards — support belonging and identity formation.

Section 03

Cultural and Diversity Contexts

Exploring the rich tapestry of cultural identities, languages, beliefs, and experiences that characterise Australian families and communities, with particular attention to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives.

Australia is one of the most culturally diverse nations in the world. Nearly 30% of the population was born overseas, and over 300 languages are spoken in Australian homes. This diversity enriches the social fabric but also creates complexities for practitioners seeking to provide culturally responsive services. Cultural contexts shape parenting beliefs, child-rearing practices, attitudes toward education, health-seeking behaviour, and family roles.

Central to any discussion of cultural contexts in Australia is the recognition of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples as the First Nations custodians of this land. Understanding the ongoing impacts of colonisation, the Stolen Generations, and systemic racism is essential for all practitioners working with children and families. Cultural contexts also include dimensions of disability, gender identity, sexual orientation, and religious belief.

Cultural disconnection and identity confusion can have serious consequences for children's mental health and wellbeing. For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children, the erosion of cultural knowledge and language threatens cultural continuity and contributes to intergenerational trauma. Refugee and migrant children may experience the tensions of acculturation, balancing heritage culture with the demands of a new society.

Conversely, strong cultural identity serves as a protective factor. Children who maintain connection to their cultural heritage demonstrate higher self-esteem, stronger family bonds, and greater resilience. Bilingualism is associated with cognitive advantages, and cultural competence in children supports empathy and respect for difference.

Key Considerations

  • •Intergenerational trauma in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities
  • •Acculturation stress for refugee and migrant families
  • •Cultural identity as a protective factor
  • •Impact of racism and discrimination on child development

Key policy frameworks include the National Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Early Childhood Strategy, the Multicultural Framework Review, and the Disability Discrimination Act. The Early Years Learning Framework (EYLF) explicitly includes cultural competence as a core practice principle, and the revised EYLF (2022) strengthens its emphasis on Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives.

The Closing the Gap initiative, while well-intentioned, has been critiqued for its deficit framing and lack of genuine partnership with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities. The National Agreement on Closing the Gap (2020) represents a shift toward shared decision-making, with four priority reforms focused on partnership, self-determination, and community-led solutions.

Culturally responsive practice moves beyond superficial celebrations of diversity to embed respect for culture in every aspect of service delivery. This includes learning about the cultural backgrounds of families in your service, adapting communication styles, incorporating cultural perspectives into curriculum, and critically reflecting on one's own cultural positionality and biases.

For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children, practice should be guided by the principles of self-determination and connection to Country. Building genuine relationships with local Elders and Aboriginal organisations, embedding Aboriginal perspectives in learning programs, and ensuring that physical environments reflect and respect Indigenous presence are all essential strategies.

Genuine relationship building
Embedding diverse perspectives

Partnerships with culturally specific organisations — such as multicultural resource centres, Aboriginal medical services, and ethnic community councils — are essential for culturally responsive practice. These organisations bring expertise, trust, and community connections that external services cannot replicate. Partnerships should be characterised by genuine collaboration rather than tokenistic consultation.

Professional development in cultural competence should be ongoing and go beyond one-off training. Programs delivered by community members themselves — such as the Aboriginal Cultural Competence Framework developed by SNAICC — provide deeper learning than generic cultural awareness training. Mentorship and community immersion experiences can further enhance practitioners' cultural understanding.

Essential resources include SNAICC's resources for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children, the EYLF and its supporting documentation, the Foundation for Young Australians' research on multiculturalism, and the Scanlon Foundation's Mapping Social Cohesion reports. Children's literature by First Nations authors — such as the works of Alison Lester collaborating with Aboriginal communities, or books by authors like Gregg Dreise — provide authentic cultural perspectives for early learning settings.

Health and Wellbeing Contexts

Investigating the physical, mental, and emotional health factors that affect children and families, and the systems that support health and wellbeing across the lifespan.

Health and wellbeing contexts encompass the full spectrum of factors that determine physical, mental, emotional, and social health. The World Health Organization's definition of health as "a state of complete physical, mental and social wellbeing and not merely the absence of disease" underscores the holistic nature of this context. For children, health is inseparable from development — optimal health provides the foundation for learning, relationships, and participation.

In Australia, significant health challenges facing children and families include rising rates of childhood obesity and related conditions, increasing mental health concerns among young people, health inequities experienced by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children and families in rural and remote areas, and the health impacts of climate change, including heat stress, air quality, and food security.

Poor health in childhood has cascading effects across the lifespan. Chronic health conditions affect school attendance, social participation, and family dynamics. The growing prevalence of mental health conditions in children — including anxiety, depression, and behavioural disorders — demands urgent attention. The COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated existing mental health concerns and disrupted access to health services, with lasting effects still being understood.

Parental health directly affects children. Maternal perinatal mental health is a particular concern, with postnatal depression affecting up to 1 in 7 new mothers in Australia. Parental chronic illness, substance use, and mental health conditions can disrupt family functioning and children's sense of security. The intergenerational transmission of trauma — particularly in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities — highlights the long-term consequences of unaddressed health needs.

Australia's health policy landscape includes Medicare (universal health insurance), the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS), and the National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Plan. Child and family health services, including maternal and child health nursing, the Healthy Kids Check, and immunisation programs, form a critical prevention and early intervention infrastructure.

Recent initiatives include the Head to Health children's mental health centres, investment in perinatal mental health screening, and the National Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health Plan. However, persistent gaps remain — particularly in mental health service availability for children under 12, in rural and remote areas, and in culturally appropriate service delivery. The Productivity Commission's 2020 report on mental health identified significant system fragmentation and called for fundamental reform.

Health-promoting practice in early childhood settings includes promoting physical activity, nutritious eating, adequate sleep, and positive social interactions. Educators should understand developmental milestones and be able to identify when a child's development may be at risk. Trauma-informed practice — which recognises the widespread impact of trauma and integrates this knowledge into all aspects of service delivery — is increasingly recognised as essential.

Mental health literacy is a critical skill for all educators. This includes understanding the signs of mental health difficulties in children, knowing how to have supportive conversations with families, and being aware of referral pathways. The Be You initiative — a national mental health in education framework — provides practical tools and professional learning for educators.

Integrated service models that co-locate health, education, and family support services show promising results. Examples include the Australian Nurse-Family Partnership Program, Child and Family Health Centres in various states, and school-based health hubs. These models reduce barriers to access and enable holistic assessment of children's and families' needs.

Partnerships with allied health professionals — occupational therapists, speech pathologists, psychologists, and dietitians — enhance the capacity of early childhood services to support children with additional health and developmental needs. Building relationships with Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Services ensures culturally safe health support for First Nations children and families.

Key resources include the Be You framework, the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW) reports on child health, the National Health and Medical Research Council's guidelines on nutrition and physical activity, and the Raising Children Network's health sections. For children, resources that promote body awareness, emotional literacy, and healthy habits — such as the Sesame Street Healthy Habits materials or the Smiling Mind mindfulness app for children — support health education in age-appropriate ways.

Crisis and Emergency Contexts

Addressing the impact of crises, emergencies, and traumatic events on children and families, and the role of practitioners in supporting recovery, resilience, and preparedness.

Crisis and emergency contexts encompass sudden-onset events — such as natural disasters (bushfires, floods, cyclones), pandemics, acts of terrorism, and community violence — as well as ongoing crises such as family domestic violence, child abuse and neglect, and refugee displacement. Australia's geographic and climatic conditions make it particularly prone to natural disasters, with the 2019–2020 Black Summer bushfires and recurring flood events highlighting the vulnerability of communities.

Children are disproportionately affected by crises. Their developmental stage makes them more vulnerable to the physical, emotional, and psychological impacts of traumatic events. They depend on adults for safety, yet adults themselves may be traumatised or overwhelmed. Understanding the developmental impact of trauma — and the factors that support resilience and recovery — is essential knowledge for all practitioners.

The impact of crises on children varies by age, developmental stage, prior experiences, and the quality of support available. Common responses include regression in behaviour (such as bedwetting or clinginess), sleep disturbances, separation anxiety, difficulties with concentration and learning, and physical symptoms such as headaches or stomach aches. For some children, trauma responses may develop into post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) or other mental health conditions.

Family functioning is often severely disrupted during crises. Loss of housing, employment, and community infrastructure can create secondary stressors that compound the primary trauma. For families experiencing domestic violence — the most common crisis context for many children — the home itself becomes a source of danger rather than safety. The cumulative impact of repeated or chronic exposure to adversity, known as adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), has well-established links to long-term health and social outcomes.

Children's Crisis Responses

  • •Behavioural regression and clinginess
  • •Sleep disturbances and nightmares
  • •Difficulty concentrating and learning
  • •Physical symptoms (headaches, stomach aches)
  • •Emotional dysregulation (anger, fear, withdrawal)

Australia has developed significant disaster management frameworks, including the National Disaster Risk Reduction Framework and the Australian Disaster Resilience Framework. The National Strategy for Disaster Resilience recognises the specific needs of children in emergencies. The Royal Commission into National Natural Disaster Arrangements (2020) made specific recommendations regarding child-centred disaster planning and recovery.

In the domain of family violence, the National Plan to End Violence against Women and Children 2022–2032 represents a coordinated national approach, with specific actions to address the needs of children as victim-survivors. Mandatory reporting requirements exist in all Australian states and territories, obligating certain professionals to report suspected child abuse and neglect. The child protection system, while critical, is widely acknowledged to be under-resourced and in need of reform, with increasing emphasis on prevention and early intervention.

In crisis contexts, practitioners must balance immediate safety needs with longer-term recovery support. Psychological first aid (PFA) — a humane, supportive, and practical response to people in acute distress — is the recommended initial approach, rather than debriefing or single-session interventions, which evidence shows may cause harm. PFA focuses on providing calm, comfort, connection to support, and information.

For ongoing crisis contexts such as domestic violence, practice must prioritise safety planning, risk assessment, and collaborative decision-making with families. Understanding the dynamics of coercive control, the barriers to leaving violent relationships, and the importance of perpetrator accountability are all essential. Self-care for practitioners is critical — working in crisis contexts carries risks of vicarious trauma, compassion fatigue, and burnout.

Crisis Response Principles

Safety first
Psychological First Aid
Collaborative decision-making
Practitioner self-care

Effective crisis response requires coordination across multiple agencies and levels of government. Emergency services, health services, education, child protection, housing, and community organisations all have roles to play. Pre-established relationships and clear communication channels are critical — crises are not the time to build partnerships from scratch.

Community-led recovery is increasingly recognised as more effective than top-down approaches. After the Black Summer bushfires, communities that had strong pre-existing social networks and local organisational capacity recovered more quickly. For practitioners, this means investing in community building during non-crisis times — strengthening connections, building local knowledge, and supporting community leadership — as a form of disaster preparedness.

Essential resources include the Australian Institute for Disaster Resilience handbook series, Emerging Minds' resources on child mental health in disasters, the Phoenix Australia centre for posttraumatic mental health resources, and state-based child protection mandatory reporting guides. For children, carefully selected picture books — such as those addressing bushfires, floods, or family changes — can support processing of traumatic experiences in developmentally appropriate ways. The Birdie's Tree books developed by the Queensland Centre for Perinatal and Infant Mental Health are specifically designed for young children affected by disasters.
